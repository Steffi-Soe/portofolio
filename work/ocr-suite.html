Case study · 2025–2026

Reading Indonesian ID cards, and catching misreads with the NIK itself

Two OCR tools that replace manual typing: one reads KTP and SIM cards for the warehouse guestbook, the other reads drivers' fuel and toll receipts for expense claims.

My role
Built both end to end
When
Oct 2025 – Aug 2026
Status
ID cards in daily use; receipts ready, waiting for the expense app
Built with
PaddleOCR, OpenCV, ONNX Runtime, Flask, FastAPI, React

The problem

At the warehouse gate, security guards copied visitor details from ID cards by hand, and only 2 fields were written down. Copying all 15 by hand, which we timed on video, takes about 159 seconds per visitor.

Drivers' fuel and toll receipts had a similar story: each one was copied onto a paper form, added up with a calculator, then typed into the system again by Finance. That's about 108 seconds of writing plus 102 seconds of typing, per receipt.

ID cards

A guard takes one photo of the card. The service cleans up the image with OpenCV, reads it with PaddleOCR's Indonesian model, then splits the text into up to 15 fields: NIK, name, place and date of birth, gender, address and so on.

The OCR is the easy part. The hard part is that it's wrong in small, believable ways: a 1 read as a 7, a smudged birth date, a place name with one letter off. So after reading, the service checks the card against itself. Place names are matched against a list of real ones, and the NIK is compared with the printed birth date and gender.

The NIK trick

A KTP's 16-digit NIK isn't random. It encodes where the card was issued and the holder's birth date, and for women, 40 is added to the day of birth. So the number tells you what the birth date and gender fields should say.

When the NIK reads cleanly, the service trusts it and uses it to correct a misread gender. When the NIK itself is garbled, it works the other way and rebuilds those digits from the printed birth date and gender. If the two still disagree, the result is marked as failing the cross-check, with a note saying which part doesn't match.

Receipts

Fuel receipts come in four layouts. The reader runs a 13-stage pipeline and extracts 8 fields, then checks the arithmetic: litres times price per litre has to equal the total. Toll receipts have no fixed layout. That reader extracts 6 fields and fuzzy-matches the toll operator and gate against lists of real names.

Making it fast enough

  • Fuel receipts: moving the OCR model to ONNX Runtime cut each scan from 2.8 to 1.8 seconds, and startup from 13 to 2.3 seconds, with identical output.
  • Toll receipts: median processing time went from 35.2 to 5.5 seconds, with no loss in accuracy.
  • SIM cards: from about 9.5 to 5.4 seconds, checked on 45 samples, with the same results.

Each speed-up was checked against the previous version's results on real samples, so a faster version couldn't quietly read differently.

What didn't work (yet)

  • Accuracy moves around. In monthly checks, field accuracy on ID cards ranged from roughly 78% to 95%, which is why I keep re-checking it every month.
  • Toll gate names are the weak spot. Across 138 hand-labelled toll receipts, 74% of fields were read correctly, but the gate name only about 45% of the time.
  • I haven't measured fuel-receipt accuracy against hand labels yet. The arithmetic check catches inconsistent reads, but it isn't the same as a labelled test set.

Results

  • Check-in dropped from about 159 seconds to about 5 per visitor, and up to 15 fields get recorded instead of 2.
  • 1,273 visitors processed from January to September 2026, saving roughly 54 hours of guards' time.
  • A 75% adoption index in an August 2026 survey of its users.
  • The ID-card service is open source on GitHub.

What I'd do next

Build a labelled test set for fuel receipts, and improve how the toll reader matches gate names.
